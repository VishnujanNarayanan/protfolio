Short answer: not from price history alone, and the interesting part is how you find that out — because a badly evaluated model will tell you it works, in a way that looks like a genuine result and survives casual review.

This is the evaluation procedure for a short-horizon price model, in the order it has to happen, with the numbers from one real attempt: 9.4 million exchange records, 31 large Indian companies, predicting whether a stock will be higher one minute from now. The headline was 61.4% accuracy against a 46.8% base rate. It was not an edge, and there are four checks that say so.

Check 0: what does the model have to beat?

Before a model exists, compute what guessing gets you. In this test data, prices went up in 46.8% of minutes. So a program that ignores every input and always answers "up" is right 46.8% of the time — that, not 50%, is the number to quote every result against. scikit-learn's DummyClassifier builds it in one line, and it should be the first row of every results table you produce.

Class balance shifts by asset and by period, so recompute it on the exact test slice you are reporting. A model quoted at "58% accurate" on a series that rises 57% of the time has done nothing.

Aggregation is a modelling decision, not preprocessing

Raw market data comes in two kinds. Trades are records of shares actually changing hands — 2.9 million here. Quotes are the prices buyers and sellers were advertising at a moment in time, whether or not anyone accepted — 6.4 million. Together, 9.4 million rows across 31 companies.

You cannot usefully predict from individual records at that level: most of what you see tick by tick is the mechanics of how an exchange matches orders, not information about where the price is going. So group by company and by minute, and collapse each group into one row — open, high, low, close, volume, and the average gap between buying and selling prices. That leaves about 11,600 rows.

Going from 9.4 million records to 11,600 is not just making the file smaller. It asserts that something predictable happens on a one-minute timescale and that anything faster is noise. It is the most consequential choice in the project and it is made before any modelling starts. Whatever your domain, write down what your aggregation window assumes and check whether the assumption is defensible, because no later result can rescue it.

Two ways to leak the answer, and the guard for each

To teach a model, each row needs an answer attached: did the price rise in the following minute? That means pulling the closing price backwards by one row.

for horizon in horizons:                       # 1, 3 and 5 minutes ahead
    merged[f'future_price_{horizon}'] = (
        merged.groupby('company')['c'].shift(-horizon))
    merged[f'future_return_{horizon}'] = (
        (merged[f'future_price_{horizon}'] - merged['c']) / merged['c'])

Guard one: group before you shift. Without groupby('company'), shifting takes the first minute of one company's day and attaches it to the last minute of another's — inventing an answer out of two unrelated series. The same applies to any panel data: users, sensors, regions.

Guard two: split by time, never at random. Hold back the last 100 minutes per company:

train = merged.groupby('company').apply(lambda x: x.iloc[:-100]).reset_index(drop=True)
test  = merged.groupby('company').apply(lambda x: x.iloc[-100:]).reset_index(drop=True)

31 companies × 100 minutes is 3,100 test rows. Neighbouring minutes share features computed from rolling windows of recent history, so a randomly chosen test row would usually have its own immediate neighbours sitting in the training data, and the model would effectively have seen the answer. Features here are averages and volatility of price and volume over the previous 3, 5, 10 and 20 minutes — computed per company so no window spans two stocks, and always backwards so none can see the future.

Those are the traps everyone warns about, and they are the easy ones. The two that actually decided this result are quieter.

Check 1: did you tune anything while watching the score?

The model does not answer "up" or "down". It produces a confidence between 0 and 1, and you choose the level at which you act. Low, and you act often on weak signals; high, and you rarely act but only on strong ones. The natural move is to try several and keep the best:

for threshold in [0.5, 0.6, 0.65, 0.7]:
    preds = (probs > threshold).astype(int)
    precision = precision_score(y_test, preds, zero_division=0)
    print(f"Threshold: {threshold}, Precision: {precision:.4f}, Trades: {preds.sum()}")

At the middle setting the model acted 1,149 times and was right 47.95% — effectively identical to always guessing up. That is the reading that should carry the most weight in the whole table. Raising the level to 0.6 gave 50.8% over 327 attempts; pushing higher made it worse than always guessing up. So this was never a quality dial being turned up. It was a shrinking sample.

The best result, 61.4% at a level of 0.63, is arithmetically correct and still misleading, because 0.63 was chosen by looking at how it scored on the very data used to judge it. Held-back data measures generalisation only for as long as you make no decisions based on it. The moment you tune against it, it measures how many things you tried. Nothing in the code leaked a future price; the leak was in the process. The fix is a three-way split — one slice to learn from, a second to choose settings on, a third opened exactly once at the end.

Check 2: how many decisions is the number actually made of?

Here is the full result at that setting:

Precision at threshold 0.63: 0.6143
Confusion Matrix:
[[1622   27]
 [1408   43]]
Predicted 1 count: 70
Actual 1 count: 1451

The model committed 70 times out of 3,100 minutes: right 43, wrong 27. With no skill you would expect about 33 right out of 70, so 43 is high — and with numbers this small, that is roughly the margin coin flips give you when they fall your way. Several confidence levels had already been tried, which makes one good-looking result far less impressive than it reads.

The other direction of that grid is more damning. There were 1,451 minutes where the price actually rose, and the model stayed silent for 1,408 of them. It catches about 3% of what it is looking for. This is not a model that predicts the market 61% of the time. It is a model that declines to answer 97.7% of the time and, on the small remainder, has an edge indistinguishable from luck.

The general rule: always report the number of decisions alongside the rate, and treat a precision figure built from fewer than a few hundred as a hypothesis rather than a result. Attach a confidence interval — for 43 out of 70 it spans roughly 49% to 73%, which contains the base rate and therefore settles nothing.

Check 3: does it survive costs?

A percentage invites you to keep tuning. Money does not. Simulating the strategy — fixed position size, every signal at the 0.6 setting, 327 trades across the test period — came out effectively flat before costs and clearly negative once realistic brokerage and transaction taxes are applied to 327 round trips.

That is the check that ends the argument, and it generalises past finance: convert the metric into the unit the decision is actually made in. Cost per false positive, hours of review per alert, money per round trip. An edge smaller than the friction is not an edge, and no accuracy figure will ever tell you that on its own.

What to do instead, in order

1. Quote the base rate first, computed on the test slice you are reporting.

2. Split three ways — train, validate, and a final set opened once. Every threshold, feature and hyperparameter decision belongs to the middle set.

3. Score on the decision unit from the start. Optimising accuracy and checking profit afterwards is backwards when one round trip costs more than the edge. Scoring each confidence level on net return would have shown most of the range underwater on the first run.

4. Test on many periods, not one. TimeSeriesSplit does this in about four lines. A single held-back window is one sample of one market mood; sliding the boundary forward and reporting the spread across windows says far more than any single number, and would have made the sample-size problem obvious immediately.

5. Match the test period to how often the model acts. If a setting fires 70 times across the whole window, the window is too short to judge it. Use more data, or restrict yourself to settings that produce enough decisions to measure.

6. Publish the negative result. The pipeline underneath one is still worth keeping — minute bars, a resumable backfill and a feature set are reusable whatever the model concludes, and the ingestion pattern that makes repeated backfills safe is in how to make an ETL pipeline safe to rerun.

References and further reading

Marcos López de Prado's Advances in Financial Machine Learning is the standard reference for testing models on financial data specifically, including the techniques for splitting time series so that overlapping windows cannot leak answers between the two halves. His chapters on how backtests mislead describe check 1 above almost exactly.

Bailey, Borwein, López de Prado and Zhu's The Probability of Backtest Overfitting puts numbers on the mistake in check 1: try enough configurations against one held-out period and a good-looking result is the expected outcome rather than evidence. It is the paper to read if you have ever tuned a threshold while watching the score.

The specific tools

  • DummyClassifier — the baseline in one line. If your model does not clearly beat it, there is nothing to discuss.
  • TimeSeriesSplit — testing across many periods instead of one, which is the smallest fix for the sample-size problem here.
  • Precision-recall curve — plotted against the 46.8% baseline it shows in one picture what my table of confidence levels took several runs to reveal: most of the curve sits at or below the line you get for free.
  • scikit-learn on cross-validation for time series — why a random split leaks when neighbouring rows share rolling-window features.
